Support shaped by your life

Younger onset dementia, disability and emerging aged care involve different lives and support arrangements. Begin with the person’s communication, abilities and chosen goals.

Living with younger onset dementia

Dementia describes conditions that affect thinking, memory and everyday functioning. Younger onset dementia means it develops before age 65; the term describes age at onset, rather than stage. Someone may still be parenting, working, managing a household or planning decades ahead. Any introduction to Aura begins with that life and the support the person wants within it. [R7]

Record identity while following present priorities

Onboarding gives the person a supported opportunity to describe themselves in their own terms. They might record their work, important people, practical knowledge, humour and hopes alongside the changes they are experiencing. A chosen supporter helps organise the material where useful, while the person reviews what represents them.

Consider someone who wants to keep attending a weekly gardening group. The starting record might include who they go with, the part of gardening they enjoy, how they prefer a reminder and what makes the journey manageable. A small activity then tests whether an agreed prompt or preparation note helps. Difficulties are brought to the appropriate practitioner and support team.

Support a particular activity

A relevant example is the British GREAT study, published in 2019. It tested cognitive rehabilitation: therapists helping people practise strategies for chosen everyday activities. Its 475 participants with mild to moderate dementia were allocated by chance to usual care or usual care plus this therapy. Ratings improved for the goals targeted in therapy, with gains maintained at nine months; broader outcomes, including cognition and quality of life, showed no significant differences between groups. Its relevance is the method of testing specific everyday goals. It did not test Aura, which needs its own evidence. [R23]

The person's own experience remains part of that evaluation. Enjoying an activity, feeling understood or expressing a preference matters even when a cognitive test is unchanged. Clinical measures answer additional questions about function, quality of life and health. The account keeps those outcomes distinguishable.

Adapt as circumstances change

Later support might use fewer choices, a different display or more help from another person. The underlying life record gives that adaptation continuity. A familiar voice or photograph may remain useful when written material becomes difficult; another person may prefer a straightforward conversation without a digital activity.

The aim is to maintain access to personally meaningful activity and better informed support through changing needs. Diagnosis, treatment, assessment of new symptoms and decisions about clinical care remain with the relevant practitioners. The personal Aura contributes context to that relationship.

Disability support shaped by the person

Disability support begins with the person's own life, abilities and chosen goals. Someone who has used communication equipment for years may know exactly how they want to express themselves. Another person may be adjusting to an acquired disability. Their existing skills and support arrangements shape the introduction to Aura.

Build on what already works

The minimum viable self includes the communication methods, sensory preferences, movement needs and practical supports relevant to the first intended functions. The person identifies which existing equipment or routines they want to keep using. Any proposed connection with other devices is checked against the actual equipment and the professional advice relevant to it.

For example, an adult might want a new support worker to understand how they indicate pain, how long they need to respond and which activities they enjoy. A short introduction drawn from reviewed records gives the worker useful context. The worker then listens to the person's present response and follows the actual support plan.

Independence includes directing assistance

A goal may involve doing a task independently, doing it with assistance or directing how another person helps. The relevant outcome follows the person's intention. An accessible planning screen might support a creative project; an audio instruction might support part of a familiar routine; a chosen summary might prepare a meeting.

The National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) funds eligible disability supports in Australia. Its Practice Standards set quality requirements for registered providers, including understandable communication, individual choice, personal values and community participation. These requirements provide a practical reference for designing and reviewing proposed Aura support. Testing asks whether the interface and activity reduce effort or add to it. [R11]

A life beyond a service category

The same person may use their Aura to organise learning, travel, friendships or an artistic project. Disability related information joins that wider account in the context where it is useful. The person chooses the material appropriate to a care encounter and the material appropriate to a social one.

Assistive technology means equipment or software helping with activities affected by disability. Claims about such help need evidence for the intended users and functions. One person's experience informs design; testing across different access needs examines dependability. A service records successful use and circumstances where another approach fits better.

The practical aim is greater agency in everyday life: expressing a choice, completing a chosen activity or receiving help in a way that makes sense to the person. Registered services and practitioners retain the duties attached to their actual roles.

Beginning support as aged care needs emerge

Early aged care support means responding to needs as they emerge, with time to understand choices and prepare. Someone might want help around the home, more social contact, support after illness or a clearer plan for a possible move. A personal Aura begins with those priorities alongside the person's existing life.

A first account that belongs to the person

Onboarding might bring together important relationships, preferred routines, communication choices, meaningful possessions and current concerns. The person decides what is relevant to the initial uses. An appointment summary or a small memory collection offers a practical way to test the relationship before a larger collection develops.

Imagine an older person preparing for an assessment while also considering downsizing. Their personal record identifies the daily activities they value and the support they already receive. They choose several questions for the assessor and a few places at home they want to record. The two activities have a shared personal context, while the assessment follows its own professional process.

Coordinate help around a meaningful goal

The World Health Organization (WHO), the United Nations health agency, publishes guidance for care workers. Its 2025 Integrated Care for Older People handbook describes assessing abilities and support needs, then coordinating a personal care plan. This is relevant to linking a goal, such as continuing to host a family meal, with practical assistance, professional advice and a review of what helped. It is a published reference for the proposal. [R13]

Chosen information is prepared for the particular encounter. A worker assisting with a routine receives the relevant preference and instructions. A family member helping with a visit receives the agreed arrangements. The personal record provides continuity while each service keeps its required records and responsibilities.

Prepare for change without putting life on hold

Recording familiar places before downsizing preserves material that would be harder to gather afterwards. Discussing future support while it is understandable also gives the person time to describe what matters. Their wishes remain open to review as circumstances and understanding change.

New experiences belong in the record too. A friendship, a different interest or an enjoyable outing gives the developing Aura present context. Whole of life support includes the ordinary pleasures that make a day worthwhile, alongside the arrangements that make daily living manageable.

My Aged Care is the government entry point for aged care assessment and services; its funding pathways are explained later. The personal starting point is a recognisable account and a useful activity through which to learn whether the support fits. [R18]

A useful next step

Begin with something that matters

Choose a familiar activity and describe what makes it manageable or enjoyable. The relevant support team can then discuss a particular change and review what helped.
